Leasehold in Bali: how it works, and what to check before signing

By the Unaya team · Updated 16 November 2026 · 7 min read

Most foreigners who “buy” a villa in Bali actually sign a leasehold: a long lease on the land. It is legal, common and solid, as long as you know what you are signing. Here are the essentials, without jargon.

What exactly is a leasehold?

A foreigner cannot fully own land in Indonesia. A leasehold (hak sewa) solves this legally: you lease the land from the Indonesian owner for a long period, through a lease signed before a notary, and you own what you build on it for that whole period.

The lease is paid upfront, in one go. That is why people talk about “buying” a leasehold: in practice, you buy the right to occupy, rent out and resell the land until the lease ends.

How long does a leasehold last?

Usually 20 to 30 years, sometimes more. What matters is not only the length but what happens at the end: a good extension option can be worth more than five extra years.

A real example: the land lease of Arawa Villas, in Balangan, runs until 21 March 2055, with an option for 30 more years at the market price at that time. When you buy an existing villa you buy the remaining years: a villa with 28 years left is not worth the same as one with 12.

What to put in the lease

  • The length and the end date, in black and white.
  • The extension: is there one? At what price (fixed in advance, indexed, or “market price”)? With how much notice?
  • The right to sublet to guests and the right to transfer the lease: without them you can neither rent out nor resell easily.
  • What happens to the building at the end: it usually goes to the landowner, unless agreed otherwise.
  • Who signs: the holder of the land certificate, and often their family. A lease signed by one person when the land belongs to several heirs is a classic source of disputes.

Checks before signing

The notary checks the land certificate at the land office (BPN), drafts and registers the deed. But that is not a full due diligence. Before signing, check:

  • Zoning: the plot must be in a zone that allows what you plan. To rent to guests, you usually look for the pink (tourism) zone on the official plan (RDTR).
  • Access: a real access road, not a path the neighbour tolerates.
  • Permits (PBG, SLF) if the villa is already built, and compliance with building rules.
  • Disputes over the land or within the owner’s family. An independent lawyer is useful here.

Taxes

Lease payments carry a 10% final tax on the landowner’s side (PPh 4(2)), often withheld at payment: check in the contract who pays it. Transfer duty (BPHTB, up to 5%) and the seller’s 2.5% tax apply to ownership transfers (freehold, HGB), not to a simple lease.

Then, if the villa is rented to guests, the tax on nightly stays (PBJT) is 10% in Badung regency.

And renting the villa to guests?

This is what changed in 2026. Since May, Bali no longer accepts new PT PMA (foreign-owned company) applications for tourist accommodation in the OSS system. A PT PMA set up today can no longer get a licence to rent out a villa by the night.

The simplest solution: hand the villa to a management company that is already licensed, such as Unaya Villa Management, which rents it out for you and pays you the income every month. And avoid at all costs buying in an Indonesian friend’s name (nominee): the property legally belongs to them, and these arrangements are banned.

Key points
  • A leasehold is a long lease (often 20–30 years) signed before a notary.
  • Extension, the right to sublet and the right to transfer must be in writing.
  • Check zoning, access, permits and disputes: the notary does not do it all.
  • To rent by the night in 2026, go through a licensed management company.
